Design notes White-hat write-up

This visualization examines the 33,358 allegation records in the supplied July 2020 police-complaint CSV. The records contain a complaint identifier, the year received, an allegation category, and a board disposition. The unit of analysis is an allegation record, not an officer, civilian, or unique complaint. Several records can belong to the same complaint, so counting rows as separate complaints would inflate that total. The chart asks a limited question: how are the recorded investigative outcomes distributed within each allegation category?

To answer it, the application groups records into the four categories supplied in the file. It combines all disposition labels beginning with “Substantiated” into one outcome, while preserving “Unsubstantiated” and “Exonerated” as separate outcomes. Within each category, each outcome count is divided by the total number of records in that category. These percentages support comparisons across categories with very different record counts. Every horizontal bar uses the same zero-to-100-percent scale, a consistent category order, and a consistent outcome order. Counts remain visible beside the bars, and tooltips report both the numerator and denominator.

The year selector filters by the year a complaint was received, not the year it closed or the date of the alleged incident. The displayed record total and percentages update together. All years are included initially; 2020 is only represented through the historical release, so it is not a complete calendar year. The three muted colors distinguish outcomes without treating unresolved evidence as a favorable finding. Text definitions and keyboard-accessible tooltips provide alternatives to relying on color alone.

The source imposes important limits. This release selects officers who were still employed in June 2020 and had at least one substantiated allegation; it is not a random sample of officers or encounters. Filed and investigated allegations cannot capture every experience of misconduct, and a board finding is not a measure of all underlying behavior. Accordingly, the chart describes this file rather than claiming a citywide misconduct rate, a causal explanation, or a trend in police conduct.

Behind the framing Black-hat write-up

The black-hat presentation uses the same complete set of allegation records as the default white-hat chart. It does not invent observations. Instead, it changes the interpretation of real outcomes. The headline announces that three in four allegations “clear officers,” and the large percentage gives that assertion an appearance of quantitative authority. The crucial step occurs before the bars are drawn: exonerated and unsubstantiated records are added together and labeled “cleared.” That creates a group of 25,057 records, or 75.1% of the file, even though only 9,609 records, or 28.8%, are actually labeled exonerated.

This aggregation erases an evidentiary distinction. An unsubstantiated outcome means the investigation could not establish whether misconduct occurred; it does not establish that the conduct was proper or the allegation false. The headline and outcome label turn uncertainty into reassurance. The supporting sentence is more restrained, making the stronger headline seem reasonable while leaving its unjustified inference intact. This combines manipulation of data transformation with manipulation of titles and labels.

The visual treatment reinforces the message. Categories are sorted from the largest combined share to the smallest, placing the strongest apparent reassurance first. A warm olive fill gives every bar a consistent, reassuring appearance. A large overall statistic, restrained typography, and a briefing-style layout make the presentation feel authoritative. The axes still run from zero to 100 percent and the arithmetic is accurate: the deception lies in what the encoded quantity is said to mean, rather than an obvious geometric distortion.

The presentation also gives the selected release a broad institutional framing that can invite unjustified generalization. It cannot establish how often all officers commit misconduct or how every complaint ends. The reveal button replaces the misleading combined percentage with the exonerated percentage, keeping the category order and denominator fixed so the effect of relabeling is visible. This chart always uses all years, independently of the white-hat filter. The surrounding educational label and this explanation disclose the deliberate manipulation; the misleading claim should not be repeated as a factual conclusion.

The takeaway

Correct arithmetic.
Incorrect implication.

Before trusting a percentage, ask what was counted, what was combined, and who is missing.

About the project

Process & transparency

Development process

The police allegations dataset was selected because its outcome categories support a direct comparison between accurate and misleading aggregation. The implementation uses CSV parsing, grouped counts, band and linear scales, SVG rectangles, data joins, transitions, dropdown filtering, and tooltips. The two charts share a parsing and summarization pipeline, but deliberately apply different outcome definitions.

The main development tasks were checking the unit of analysis and category totals, designing the contrasting readings, implementing the interactions, and writing the explanations. The reveal interaction isolates the effect of changing the outcome definition while holding the records and denominator constant.

Team contributions

Deep Mistry and Jack Perlman developed this project collaboratively. The assignment’s allowance for AI-assisted coding gave us the flexibility to explore two different directions before choosing a final approach. Jack experimented with visualizations of the census-tract gentrification dataset, while Deep explored the police allegations dataset. We then peer-reviewed each other’s work, comparing how effectively each approach communicated the data and demonstrated the contrast between white-hat and black-hat visualization. Extra credit work

The “Reveal the missing distinction” control turns the black-hat visualization into an interactive demonstration of how aggregation can change a story. Initially, the chart combines unsubstantiated and exonerated allegations under the misleading label “cleared,” producing an overall figure of 75.1%. Clicking the control switches the calculation to exonerated allegations alone, reducing that figure to 28.8%. The bars transition to their corrected lengths, and the headline, overall percentage, outcome labels in tooltips, and explanatory caption update together. An accompanying explanation identifies the missing 46.3% as unsubstantiated allegations: cases where the evidence did not establish whether misconduct occurred.

The comparison deliberately keeps the underlying records, category order, zero-to-100-percent scale, and denominator for each category fixed. Only the outcome grouping and its framing change. This lets viewers isolate the effect of combining categories instead of wondering whether a different sample or a rescaled axis caused the change. The control is reversible, so viewers can compare the misleading and corrected versions repeatedly. Tooltips provide exact counts and percentages in both states, and keyboard access and reduced-motion support make the interaction usable without requiring a mouse or animation.

This feature goes beyond presenting the two required visualizations and explaining them in text: it adds a directly inspectable correction within the misleading chart itself. Viewers can see how mathematically accurate numbers support an unjustified conclusion when uncertainty is relabeled as exoneration. The educational contribution is this controlled, reversible comparison, rather than animation alone.
